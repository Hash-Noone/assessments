# Py-Section 1 Mission Project: The Digital Translator

## Tier 1: The Literal Assistant ("Make me breakfast")

I picked a common Nigerian breakfast: boiled yam with egg sauce and a cup of hot tea. The robot only does exactly what it's told, so I had to spell out every single move, including how it knows each thing is done.

**1. Find the kitchen.**

1.1 Stop where you are. Turn slowly all the way around (360°) and take note of every doorway, wall, piece of furniture and obstacle you can see.

1.2 Work out if you're already in the kitchen. The kitchen is the room with a refrigerator, a stove and a sink. If you can see all three, skip ahead to step 1.6.

1.3 If you can't, pick the closest doorway you haven't been through yet. Before you move, make sure the way there is clear: no people, pets, things on the floor, closed doors or steps. If something's in the way, go around it. If you can't get around it, pick a different doorway.

1.4 Walk slowly through the doorway. Stop just after it and repeat steps 1.1 and 1.2 in the new room.

1.5 If you've checked every room and still haven't found the kitchen, stop and tell me "I cannot find the kitchen."

1.6 Find the refrigerator. It's the tall, box-shaped appliance, usually with one or two handled doors, and it's cold inside. If there's more than one tall appliance, go with the one that has shelves of food and a light when you open it.

**2. Get to the refrigerator.**

2.1 Look along the straight line between you and the refrigerator and check for anything in the way, like chairs, open cabinet doors, things on the floor or people.

2.2 Pick a route that avoids all of them. If there's no way through at all, stop and tell me what's blocking you.

2.3 Walk along that route and check what's ahead of you with every step. If something moves into your path, wait until it's clear again.

2.4 Stop about one arm's length (60 cm) from the refrigerator, facing the doors.

**3. Open the refrigerator.**

3.1 Find the handle on the door. If there are two doors (top and bottom, or left and right), use the bigger one that's cold inside. That's the fridge, not the freezer.

3.2 Make sure nothing is in the space the door will swing into. If the door would hit you, step to the side.

3.3 Hold the handle with one hand and pull it smoothly towards you until the door is all the way open (about 90°).

3.4 Check that the light inside is on and you can see the shelves. If the door didn't open, give it one more, firmer pull. If it still won't open, stop and tell me.

**4. Check that everything is there before you touch anything.** This has to happen before anything is taken out, cut or heated. If something is missing, nothing has been wasted and nothing is left switched on.

4.1 On the middle shelf, open the egg carton and count the eggs. There must be at least 3.

4.2 In the vegetable drawer at the bottom of the fridge, check there is at least 1 large red tomato, 1 fresh pepper (tatashe or rodo) and 1 onion.

4.3 Close the refrigerator door.

4.4 In the basket on the floor beside the counter, check there is a tuber of yam at least 20 cm long.

4.5 On the shelf above the stove, check there is vegetable oil in the bottle (lift it: an empty bottle weighs almost nothing), salt in the salt container, at least 1 tea bag in the tea box, sugar in the sugar container and at least 2 heaped teaspoons of powdered milk in the milk tin.

4.6 Check that the gas cylinder valve beside the cooker is turned on (the handle points in line with the pipe). If it isn't, turn it until it does.

4.7 If any of these checks fail, stop and tell me exactly which item is missing (for example, "There are only 2 eggs."). Don't do any of the steps below.

**5. Get out the equipment.** From the lower-left cabinet, take the medium pot with a lid (about 3 litres) and the small frying pan (20 cm). From the top drawer, take the kitchen knife, 1 fork, 1 tablespoon, 1 teaspoon and the wooden spoon. From the upper cabinet above the sink, take the chopping board, 1 small bowl, 1 plate and 1 mug. Put everything on the counter beside the stove.

**6. Cut and peel the yam.**

6.1 Put the yam on the chopping board. Hold the yam with your left hand, keeping your fingers at least 5 cm away from where the knife will cut.

6.2 Starting from the thinner end, cut 4 round slices, each about 2 cm thick. Put the rest of the tuber back in the basket.

6.3 Peel each slice: hold it flat on the board and cut away the brown skin all the way around, cutting downwards, away from your hand. A slice is peeled when no brown skin is left, only white or cream flesh.

6.4 Cut each peeled slice in half, so you have 8 pieces.

**7. Boil the yam.**

7.1 Rinse the yam pieces under the cold tap until no sand or dirt is left on them, then put them in the pot.

7.2 Fill the pot with cold water from the tap until the water is 3 cm above the top of the yam. Add 1 level teaspoon of salt.

7.3 Put the pot on the front-left burner and put the lid on.

7.4 The burner knobs are marked OFF, then 1 to 9, where 1 is the lowest flame and 9 is the highest. Push in the front-left knob and turn it to 9 to light it. Check that a blue flame is burning under the pot. If no flame appears within 5 seconds, turn the knob back to OFF, wait 1 minute for the gas to clear, and try once more. If it still doesn't light, turn it to OFF, stop and tell me.

7.5 When you see bubbles breaking all over the surface of the water (the water is boiling), turn the knob down to 5. That is what I mean by medium.

7.6 After 15 minutes, take off the lid and push the fork into the thickest piece of yam. The yam is done when the fork slides all the way in with no resistance. If you feel resistance, put the lid back, wait 3 more minutes and test again. If the yam pieces start breaking apart in the water, it's overcooked, so go straight to 7.7.

7.7 Turn the front-left knob to OFF. Hold the lid slightly open over the pot with a dry cloth, tilt the pot over the sink and pour out all the water, keeping the yam in the pot. Put the pot back on the cold burner with the lid on to keep the yam warm.

**8. Prepare the vegetables for the egg sauce.**

8.1 Open the refrigerator again (the same way as in step 3). Take out 3 eggs, 1 tomato, 1 pepper and 1 onion, put them on the counter and close the door.

8.2 Rinse the tomato and pepper under the tap. On the chopping board, cut the tomato, the pepper (with its seeds and stem removed) and half of the onion (peeled) into small pieces, no bigger than 1 cm across. Keep your fingers away from the blade, as in step 6.1. Put the leftover half onion back in the fridge vegetable drawer.

8.3 Crack the 3 eggs on the edge of the small bowl and empty them into it. Throw the shells in the bin under the sink. If any shell falls into the bowl, fish it out with the fork. Add a pinch of salt and beat with the fork for 30 seconds, until the yellow and white are fully mixed.

**9. Make the egg sauce.**

9.1 Put the frying pan on the front-right burner. Pour in 2 tablespoons of vegetable oil.

9.2 Light the front-right burner the same way as in step 7.4, then turn it to 5 (medium). Wait 1 minute for the oil to heat. The oil is ready when it moves easily across the pan when you tilt it slightly. It must not be smoking. If it smokes, turn the knob down to 3.

9.3 Add the onion pieces. Stir with the wooden spoon every 10 seconds for 1 minute, until the onion looks soft and see-through at the edges.

9.4 Add the tomato and pepper pieces and a pinch of salt. Stir every 30 seconds for 5 minutes. The vegetables are ready when the tomato pieces have broken down into a thick sauce and you can see oil separating at the edge of the pan.

9.5 Pour the beaten eggs over the vegetables. Leave them for 20 seconds, then stir gently every 10 seconds so the eggs and vegetables mix.

9.6 The egg sauce is done when you can't see any runny, shiny egg anywhere in the pan (about 2 to 3 minutes). Then turn the front-right knob to OFF.

**10. Make the tea.**

10.1 Fill the kettle with water from the tap up to the "1 cup" line (or about 300 ml). Put it on its base and switch it on. Wait until it switches itself off and you can see steam coming from the spout.

10.2 Put 1 tea bag, 1 level teaspoon of sugar and 2 heaped teaspoons of powdered milk into the mug.

10.3 Pour the hot water into the mug until it's 2 cm below the rim. Stir with the teaspoon for 15 seconds, until no milk powder is floating on top.

10.4 Leave the tea bag in for 2 minutes, then lift it out with the teaspoon and throw it in the bin. The tea is ready when it's an even light-brown colour all through.

**11. Serve.**

11.1 Use the fork to put all 8 pieces of yam on the left half of the plate.

11.2 Use the wooden spoon to put all the egg sauce on the right half of the plate.

11.3 Carry the plate, a clean fork and the mug of tea to the dining table and set them down in front of the chair I'm sitting in. Make sure the mug handle faces my right hand.

**12. Make the kitchen safe.**

12.1 Look at every burner knob. Each one must point to OFF and there must be no flame on the cooker.

12.2 Turn the gas cylinder valve off (the handle points across the pipe).

12.3 Check the refrigerator door is shut by pulling the handle gently: it should not swing open.

12.4 Say "Breakfast is ready."

The job is done when the plate of boiled yam and egg sauce and the mug of tea are on the table in front of me, every burner is OFF, the gas valve is closed, and the leftover food is back where it came from.

### Reasoning challenge: what "Make me breakfast" quietly assumes

When I wrote this out, I realised how much a simple sentence like "Make me breakfast" leaves out:

- What breakfast it should be (yam and egg? bread and tea? akara and pap?) and how much of it.
- Where the food, equipment and utensils actually are. The yam isn't in the fridge, and the tea things are on a shelf.
- That the ingredients are even there. A person glances around the kitchen first without thinking about it. The machine had to be told to check before it started cutting or cooking, not halfway through.
- Which equipment to use, and what the settings mean. "Medium heat" means nothing to a machine unless you say what the scale on the knob is.
- How to use the equipment safely: lighting a gas burner, holding a knife, draining hot water, and turning the gas off at the end.
- What order to do things in. The yam goes on first because it takes the longest, and the fridge has to be opened again before anything can come out of it.
- How to know when something is finished. "Cooked" had to turn into "the fork slides in with no resistance" for the yam and "no runny egg in the pan" for the sauce.
- Where to serve it, and the fact that cleaning up and switching things off is part of the job.
- What to do when something goes wrong, like a missing ingredient or a burner that won't light.
- Even "me" and "now" are assumptions: who the breakfast is for, and when they want it.

A person fills in all of these gaps without thinking, from context and habit. A machine that follows instructions literally can't do that, so every one of them had to be written down.

## Tier 2: Algorithm Thinking (Withdrawing money from an ATM)

**Problem:** Withdraw money from an ATM.

**Algorithm:**

1. **Check the bank connection, then wait for a card.** Before accepting a card, the ATM checks that it can reach the bank. If it can't, it shows "Temporarily out of service," doesn't accept cards, and tries the connection again every minute. Once it's connected, it shows "Insert card." The ATM first needs to know who the customer is.
2. **Read the card.** Read the chip or magnetic stripe to get the account number and the bank.
   - *If the card can't be read:* show "Card cannot be read," give the card back and go back to step 1.
   - *If the card has expired or been reported stolen or blocked:* show a message and keep the card (or give it back, depending on the bank's policy), then end.
3. **Ask for the PIN.** Show "Enter your PIN" and hide the digits as they're typed. Having the card shows you hold it. The PIN shows you're the owner.
4. **Check the PIN with the bank.** The count of wrong attempts is kept by the bank, against the card, not by the ATM. If the ATM kept it, someone could pull the card out and put it back in to reset the count, and get unlimited guesses. The count only goes back to 0 after a correct PIN.
   - *If the PIN is wrong:* the bank adds 1 to the card's failed-attempts count. If there have been fewer than 3 wrong attempts, show "Incorrect PIN, try again" and go back to step 3. On the 3rd wrong attempt, the bank blocks the card, the ATM keeps it, shows "Card retained, contact your bank," and ends.
5. **Show the menu and let the customer pick "Withdrawal."** ATMs can do other things too, like checking a balance or making a transfer, so the machine has to know what the customer wants.
6. **Choose the account** (for example savings or current), if the card is linked to more than one.
7. **Enter the amount.** Check that it's valid. It has to be more than 0, a multiple of the smallest note the machine holds (for example 1,000), and within the per-transaction or daily limit.
   - *If the amount isn't valid:* explain why and go back to step 7.
8. **Check the available balance with the bank.**
   - *If the amount is more than the available balance:* show "Insufficient funds" and offer to enter a smaller amount (step 7) or cancel.
9. **Check the cash in the ATM.** Can the machine make up the exact amount with the notes it has?
   - *If it doesn't have enough cash or the right notes:* show "This ATM cannot dispense that amount; maximum available is X," then go back to step 7 or cancel.
10. **Reserve the money.** Ask the bank to put a hold (or debit) on the amount. Doing this before handing out cash means nobody can walk away with money the bank hasn't recorded.
11. **Give the card back** and wait for the customer to take it. Returning the card before the cash makes people less likely to forget their card. There is a trade-off, though: once the card is out, the session is half closed while the machine can still fail at the next step. That's why the failure handling in step 12 doesn't depend on the card being in the machine. The hold from step 10 is enough to undo the transaction.
12. **Dispense the cash,** counting the notes as they come out.
    - *If the transaction can't be finished* (the cash jams, the power or network goes down, or the customer doesn't take the cash within about 30 seconds): cancel or reverse the hold so the customer isn't charged for money they never got. Log the error, show "Transaction cancelled, your account has not been charged," and alert the bank so the machine gets serviced.
13. **Update the account.** Confirm the debit with the bank so the balance goes down by the amount withdrawn.
14. **Print or show a receipt** (optional) with the amount and the new balance.
15. **Show "Thank you"** and go back to step 1.

**At any point:**

- If the customer presses Cancel or doesn't respond within a set time, give the card back, undo anything that wasn't finished, and go back to step 1.
- If the connection to the bank drops partway through (for example during step 4 or 8), show "Transaction cannot be completed," give the card back, undo anything not finished, and go back to step 1, which won't accept a new card until the connection is back.

**Why each part is there:** the ATM has to make sure it can reach the bank (connection check), identify the customer (card), confirm it's really them (PIN), understand what they want (menu and amount), check that it's possible (balance and cash), carry it out safely (hold and dispense), record it (update the account), and then finish cleanly (card, receipt and reset).

**Problem, algorithm, program:**

- **Problem:** "Withdraw money from an ATM." This is the goal.
- **Algorithm:** the ordered steps and failure checks above. It's the plan for solving the problem, written in plain language.
- **Program:** the same steps written in a programming language that the ATM's computer can actually run.

## Tier 3: Input, Processing, Output (The simple calculator)

| Operation      | Input | Processing | Output |
|----------------|-------|------------|--------|
| Addition       | 20, 5 | 20 + 5     | 25     |
| Subtraction    | 20, 5 | 20 − 5     | 15     |
| Multiplication | 20, 5 | 20 × 5     | 100    |
| Division       | 20, 5 | 20 ÷ 5     | 4      |

**How the calculator works (design):**

1. **Input:** get `number_1`, `number_2` and the operation the user picked (+, −, ×, ÷).
2. **Check the input:** make sure both inputs are numbers and the operation is one of the four. If not, show "Invalid input" and ask again.

   This is where most of the real work is. Whatever the user types arrives as text. In Python, `input()` always returns a string, so typing 20 gives the program `"20"`, not the number 20. The check means trying to convert each string into a number (for example with `float()`). If the conversion works, the input is a number. If it fails (say the user typed "twenty" or left it blank), the input is invalid.
3. **Check that the operation is possible:** if the operation is division and `number_2` is 0, show an error and ask for a different second number (see below).
4. **Process:** carry out the chosen operation on `number_1` and `number_2`.
5. **Output:** show the result.

### Reasoning challenge: 20 ÷ 0

Dividing by zero doesn't have an answer, because there's no number you can multiply by 0 to get 20. If the program just tries it anyway, it will crash or stop with an error (in Python that's a `ZeroDivisionError`). Some systems give back a meaningless value instead, like "infinity" or "NaN".

A well-designed program checks the input before it does any processing. If the operation is division and `number_2` is 0, it shouldn't even try. It should show a clear message like "Error: cannot divide by zero. Please enter a different second number," and let the user try again, instead of crashing or giving a wrong answer. The general rule is to validate the input, deal with impossible cases on purpose, and tell the user what went wrong.

## Tier 4: Data Translator

| Information | What kind of information it is     | Typical data type |
|-------------|------------------------------------|-------------------|
| Victor      | Text                               | string (`str`) |
| 30          | Whole number                       | integer (`int`) |
| 150000.50   | Number with a decimal part (money) | exact decimal (`Decimal`) or a whole number of kobo (`int`), not `float` |
| Yes         | A true/false state                 | boolean (`bool`: `True`) |

**Why each one needs its own type:**

- "Victor" is a sequence of characters. We use it to display and match names, not to do maths with.
- 30 is a quantity, so we can compare it or calculate with it (for example, "is the customer over 18?").
- 150000.50 is a quantity with a fractional part, and because it's money it has to be stored exactly. That rules out `float`. A float stores numbers in binary, and most decimal fractions (like 0.10 or 0.50 of something) can't be written exactly in binary, so the computer keeps a very close approximation instead. In Python, `0.1 + 0.2` gives `0.30000000000000004`. One tiny error doesn't look like much, but a bank adds and subtracts millions of amounts, and the errors build up into real money going missing. So money is stored either as an exact `Decimal` or as a whole number of the smallest unit (kobo), where there's no fraction to get wrong.
- "Yes" only has two possible states (active or inactive), so it's stored as true or false. The program can then test it in a condition like "if the account is active...".

**How each one becomes digital:**

- **Victor:** a character encoding (Unicode/ASCII) turns each letter into a number (V = 86, i = 105, c = 99, t = 116, o = 111, r = 114), and each number is stored in binary (V = 01010110, and so on).
- **30:** stored as a binary integer, 00011110.
- **150000.50:** stored as a whole number of kobo. ₦150,000.50 is 15,000,050 kobo, and that integer is stored in binary.
- **Yes:** becomes `True`. In principle a true/false value only needs one bit (1 for true, 0 for false). In practice computers can't address a single bit on its own, so it takes up at least a whole byte, and in Python `True` is actually a full object in memory. The idea of "one bit" is still a good way to think about how little information it holds.

So the journey is: real-world information, then a data type, then numbers, then binary (0s and 1s), and finally storage in memory or on disk.

### Reasoning challenge: what if the balance is read wrongly?

Bits don't mean anything on their own. The computer has to read them using the right type and format. If the balance is stored or read the wrong way (say the decimal point gets lost and 150000.50 turns into 15,000,050, the bits get read as the wrong type, or there's a rounding or overflow error), the system will treat a completely different amount as the real balance.

That could cause a lot of problems:

- The customer might see the wrong balance, or be allowed to withdraw money they don't have, and the bank loses money.
- Perfectly valid transactions could be turned down for "insufficient funds" even though the customer has the money.
- Interest, fees and statements would all be worked out from the wrong number, and the mistake would carry into every transaction and report after it.
- The bank could lose customers' trust and face complaints, plus legal and regulatory trouble.

## Tier 5: Explain the Whole System (The online store)

**Scenario:** a customer orders 2 of a product that costs ₦50,000 from an online store.

**1. Customer input.** The customer chooses the product, sets the quantity to 2, types in their delivery address and enters their payment details. All of this is data coming into the system. On its own it's just text and numbers the customer typed, like the `"20"` in the calculator.

**2. Application / program.** The online store's program receives that input. The program is what turns "a customer wants this" into something the computer can act on. Without it, the computer would have the data but no idea what to do with it.

**3. Instructions.** The program tells the computer to:

- check the input is valid (a real product, a quantity above 0, a complete address)
- look up the product and check that at least 2 are in stock
- work out the order total
- charge the payment and wait for the payment provider to approve it
- reduce the stock by 2 and save the order
- send the customer a confirmation

Like the ATM, it also needs to know what to do when something fails. If only 1 is in stock, it tells the customer before taking any money. If the payment is declined, it doesn't save the order or reduce the stock, and it asks the customer to try another card.

**4. Data processing.** The computer carries out those instructions on the data:

```
Product price:   50,000
Quantity:        2
-----------------------
Order total:    100,000
```

This is the same input, processing, output pattern as the calculator, just inside a bigger system. The price should be stored exactly (as `Decimal` or in kobo), for the same reason as the bank balance in Tier 4.

**5. Stored data.** The system has to remember the order after the page closes, so it saves a record like this:

| Field        | Example                | Data type |
|--------------|------------------------|-----------|
| Customer     | Victor                 | string |
| Product      | (product ID)           | integer or string |
| Quantity     | 2                      | integer |
| Order amount | 100,000.00             | Decimal / integer kobo |
| Order status | Paid, awaiting delivery | string |

It also saves the new stock level. In the end, all of this is stored as binary on a disk, just like Victor's details in Tier 4.

**6. Result / output.** The customer sees "Order confirmed. Total: ₦100,000." That message is output, produced from the data the system just processed and stored.

### Final challenge: the whole chain in my own words

Customer → **input data** (product, quantity, address, payment) → **program** (the online store's instructions) → **processing** (checks, total, payment, stock update) → **stored data** (the order record and new stock level) → Result (the order confirmation)

- **What is the computer doing?** It's running the program's instructions one by one, on the data it's been given. It doesn't know what "buying something" means. It's just reading, comparing, calculating and storing values.
- **What is the program doing?** It's the translation of the store's business rules (check stock, charge the right amount, record the order) into steps the computer can carry out, including what to do when something goes wrong.
- **What data is involved?** The customer's choices and details, the product's price and stock level, the payment result, the calculated total, and the order status.
- **What instructions are being executed?** Validate the input, check stock, calculate the total, take payment, reduce stock, save the order, and send the confirmation.
- **What is the output?** The confirmation message, plus things the customer doesn't see directly, like a receipt email and an updated stock level.
- **What needs to be stored?** Who ordered, what they ordered, how many, how much they paid, where it's going and the order's status, so the store can deliver it, the customer can track it, and the stock count stays correct.

The stages only work together. Input is useless without a program to process it, the program can't do anything sensible unless the data is represented correctly, and the result only means something if the order was stored so the system can act on it later.

## Final Reflection

*"A computer does not understand the real world directly. It works with representations of information and follows instructions for processing those representations."*

To me, this means a computer never deals with the actual thing, only with a stand-in for it that someone chose. It doesn't know what an egg, a customer or money is. It has numbers that represent them, and steps someone wrote telling it what to do with those numbers. All of the meaning comes from the people who designed the representation and wrote the instructions.

Every example in this project shows that:

- **Breakfast:** the machine didn't understand "breakfast" or "cooked." I had to turn those into things it could actually check, like "at least 3 eggs in the carton," "the fork slides into the yam with no resistance" and "no runny egg left in the pan."
- **ATM:** the machine doesn't know who you are. The card and PIN are representations of your identity, and the balance is a representation of your money. The algorithm has to handle every way those can fail.
- **Calculator:** the program doesn't even receive the number 20. It gets the text `"20"` and has to be told to turn it into a number, and told what to do when the input makes no sense, like dividing by zero.
- **Customer data:** "Victor," 30, 150000.50 and "Yes" all end up as binary, and they only mean the right thing if they're read back with the right type. Read the balance wrongly and the bank works with an amount that was never real.
- **Online store:** an order is just a set of stored values and a series of instructions that change them. The customer sees "a purchase," but the computer only sees data being checked, calculated and saved.

So being a "digital translator" means doing the understanding the computer can't do: working out exactly what the real-world task needs, choosing how to represent the information, and writing instructions precise enough that the computer gets the right result without having to guess.
